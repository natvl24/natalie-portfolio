Strategy

Objective

Own the occasion, not the season.

Occasionwear is often bought with a date on the calendar. The campaign's job is to make Abercrombie one of the first places people think of when an invitation arrives, which means showing up across occasions year-round rather than relying on one seasonal push.

Insight

People don't just search for a dress. They search for what the dress code means.

“Black tie optional,” “garden party,” and “cocktail attire” are often the starting point. Helping someone figure out what to wear makes the content useful, saveable, and easy to send to the group chat.

Big idea

Every invitation has an outfit.

Creators aren't just handed products. They're given an occasion and a dress code to solve. The content follows them choosing, styling, and wearing the look, positioning Abercrombie as the answer to whatever is next on the calendar.

Pillars

Decode · GRWM · Try-on · Repost

Dress-code guides make the campaign useful and saveable. GRWMs bring the outfits into real occasions. Multi-size try-ons help shoppers understand fit, and UGC reposts show how the collection looks on the people actually wearing it.

Casting

Nano · 1K–10K Volume & trust

The bulk of the roster. Real guests at real weddings, cast by city. Highest comment-to-view ratio and the cheapest way to look everywhere at once.

Micro · 10K–100K Craft & conversion

Fashion and lifestyle creators who can shoot a clean try-on. These are the assets worth putting paid spend behind.

Mid · 100K–500K Reach & permission

A small number, used to set the campaign's tone and give the smaller tiers something to respond to. Never the whole plan.

Brief Loose on purpose

One mandated beat (the dress code named out loud), one product link, everything else theirs. Over-briefing is what makes creator content look like an ad.

Design decisions

Type

Invitation typography, not retail typography.

A high-contrast serif anchors headlines like “Cocktail,” “Garden,” and “Dress Codes, Decoded,” while small, wide-tracked capitals reference the typography of formal invitations. The contrast gives the content an editorial, etiquette-inspired feel rather than making it read like a traditional promotion.

Color

Navy type sits on a consistent cream background, while tan and chocolate tones come through the clothing rather than the layout itself. Keeping the campaign on one neutral field creates visual consistency across covers, slides, squares, and Stories, while letting the garments remain the primary source of color.

Imagery

Editorial occasion photography on cream.

One look per frame, photographed in a setting that reflects the invitation, a formal venue, a garden, or the beach at golden hour. Each image is set within the same cream field so the four dress codes read as one cohesive series, while getting-ready imagery is reserved for Stories, where a more intimate, anticipatory format feels natural to the platform.

Layout

Structure holds at every size.

The wordmark stays in the top left, the dress code sits in the upper margin, and the headline and model split the frame. That same structure carries across 4:5, 1:1, and 9:16 formats so the campaign feels consistent on every platform. Numbering like “01 / 04” helps the assets read as a series, while keeping each dress code on screen makes the content easy to understand without sound and easier to scan quickly.

Copy

"Occasionwear for every invitation."

The campaign line focuses on the occasion rather than the garment, while each piece of copy has a clear purpose. “Dress Codes, Decoded” gives people something useful to save, “GRWM: Wedding Guest Edition” immediately sets the format, and “Which look would you wear?” invites interaction. Since occasion shopping can feel overwhelming, the copy is meant to make choosing what to wear feel simpler and more approachable.

Format

One layout, three formats.

4:5 is used for covers and saveable carousels, 1:1 for casting and strategy boards, and 9:16 for Stories, polls, and swipe-up moments. Each format is designed for how it will actually be used on the platform, rather than resizing the same asset for every placement. Paid support would be considered for the strongest-performing organic creative.

Measurement

Reach
By tier, so cost per tier is comparable
Engagement
Comments weighted over likes
Saves
The decoder's real KPI
CTR
Per creator link, not campaign average
Conversion
Creator-attributed revenue vs fee

Creator performance is scored individually and the roster is re-cut every cycle: promote what converts, cut what only reaches. Targets would be set against the brand's own baseline, not against a category benchmark — I'd rather beat last quarter than an industry average.
