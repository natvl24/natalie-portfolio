The brief

Situation

A year in, and the club was still known event by event.

Fifty-plus events had built a real audience but no single moment anyone outside it could point to. The anniversary was the chance to make the club legible to venues, brands and people who'd never come.

Objective

Fill the biggest room we'd ever booked — and make money doing it.

Earlier events had taught me that a full room isn't the same as a profitable one, so this one started as a cost model: venue, staff, product, print, then the ticket price that had to clear it.

Audience

The 6.6K who follow, and the friend they'd bring.

Existing members were the guaranteed base. The growth came from designing the night so bringing someone was the obvious move rather than an afterthought.

The idea

Celebrate the members, not the brand.

Every asset was built from a year of real photos of real attendees. An anniversary that congratulates itself is an ad; one that shows the people who made it is an invitation with proof attached.

The creative

Every flyer, story frame, calendar board and recap post below is mine, built in Canva off one template kit so a new asset takes twenty minutes and still looks like us.

The year that led to it

Partnerships

The pitch

A room full of people the partner actually wants to reach.

Venues gained access to an engaged local audience, while brands got their products into guests’ hands, social content they could repost, and exposure to a clearly defined community. I created the partnership deck, led outreach, and negotiated the terms myself.

What it built

65+ venues, 45 brands, $1.2K in funding.

Across the year, I built partnerships with 65+ businesses to host events and 45 brands through product, giveaways, and co-promotion. I also secured funding from a social-connection app startup and served as its New York point of contact for community growth and app adoption. innisfree was one of the largest sponsors of the anniversary event, providing branded totes and products for guests.

The follow-up

A recap deck within a week, every time.

After each partnership, I sent attendance, social reach, photos, and event highlights while the activation was still fresh. That follow-up helped turn one-off collaborations into ongoing relationships and made future partnership conversations much easier.

Design decisions

Type

A bold display headline on every asset.

Every flyer leads with the event name set large and heavy — Bouquet Swap, Wine Wednesday, Flower Hour — with date, time and venue underneath in small caps, and script or handwritten type used only for the warm line at the top or bottom. Locking that hierarchy early is what let volume happen: fifty events a year is only possible if I'm not redesigning the layout every week.

Color

Pink and green are the club's own colors, used on the wordmark, the thank-you cards and the recap posts. Individual events then get their own palette drawn from the occasion — deep red for wine nights, blue and pink for the 5K, green stripes for dinner — always at full saturation and always with the same type and framing, so the set reads as one club running many nights rather than one template recolored.

Imagery

Illustration on the flyer, real faces in the recap.

Promotion runs on simple illustration and vintage-print motifs — a bouquet, a wine glass, a football — because a drawing sets the tone without promising who will be in the room. The recaps then do the opposite: candid photos of actual members and actual nights, no stock and no staged shoots, because the question in a guest's head is “would I fit in here,” and only real faces answer it.

Layout

The monthly corkboard.

Name at the top, artwork in the middle, logistics in a fixed block at the bottom — the same skeleton across every flyer, invite and card. The monthly events board is the exception and the signature: one collage carrying the whole calendar, built to be saved and re-shared rather than scrolled. A post people return to beats a post people like.

Copy

The plan already exists. Just come.

The name is the thesis: come for the story, not the networking. Lines like “Thanks a bunch” and “You helped this bouquet bloom” do the thanking, and every event line is written to make showing up alone feel normal and to survive being screenshotted to a friend with no context.

Format

Stories sell, the grid archives, print hosts.

The 4:5 flyers are built for the grid and the story repost, the events board is built to be saved, and the thank-you cards, cocktail menus and wine journals are printed for the table — with QR codes on the cards so the night before flows into the next RSVP. Same system, made for where it actually lands.

What I learned

Price from the model

I priced early events on instinct and learned that a sold-out room can still lose money. Now every event starts with a cost model, and the ticket price comes from the economics rather than the other way around.

Own the list

I relied too heavily on social platforms to reach the same people again instead of building an email list from the start. If I could redo one thing, I’d capture direct audience data from day one so growth wasn’t dependent on the algorithm.

Consistency scales, novelty doesn't

The repeatable pieces, like recurring event formats, the corkboard, and the recap deck, did more for long-term growth than any one-off idea. Building systems made it possible to keep producing events consistently without reinventing the process every time.
